What is the difference between optimistic and pessimistic locking? What is the conflict window, and why does it decide everything? The zeroth question: do you need a lock at all? The Claim Test: how do senior engineers choose optimistic vs pessimistic locking? Question 1 — can you hold the lock for the whole window? Optimistic vs pessimistic locking is a choice about when you check for a conflicting write, not about whether you lock. Pessimistic locking claims the row up front with a database lock, so no one else can touch it until you commit. Optimistic locking lets the write proceed and checks a version number at commit time, failing if another transaction changed the row first.

Start optimistic, and move to pessimistic only when conflicts are frequent and the work you would lose is expensive to redo. That one sentence settles more arguments than most code reviews. The trouble is that almost every guide on the topic starts in the wrong place. It opens with "optimistic is faster, pessimistic is safer," picks a side, and never explains the question underneath. Here is the problem both strategies exist to solve. Two requests read the same row — an account balance, an inventory count, a document — at the same time. Each one computes a new value from what it read. The second write silently overwrites the first, and the update from the first request is simply gone.

This is sometimes described as breadcrumbing : intermittent signals of interest or affection that maintain a sense of connection without leading to meaningful relational progression. The term is often used to describe deliberate manipulation, but that's not always what is happening. An emotionally unavailable person may not be consciously trying to keep you attached. They may genuinely miss you or want some form of connection while simultaneously being unable to tolerate, sustain, or pursue the level of intimacy that a real relationship requires. The effect can still be destabilizing and keep you preoccupied with the relationship. Why a small signal can have such a large effect When you are attached to someone, their messages don't arrive in a psychological vacuum. A simple "I miss you" can activate memories of months or years of intimacy, sex, private jokes, emotional disclosures, imagined futures, and the feeling of being deeply understood. The message may contain only three words, but your brain supplies the entire history.

Every Android phone, every cloud server, every supercomputer on the planet depends on a single kernel subsystem to allocate, reclaim, and protect its memory. 17.9% of all Linux kernel security vulnerabilities between 2020 and 2024 came from this code. One person reviewed the code going into this subsystem for 26 years. On April 21, 2026, that person posted a message to the kernel mailing list saying he intended to begin stepping away.

Two weeks later, at a developer summit in Zagreb, the memory management team tried to figure out how to replace him. The Colonel of the Kernel His name is Andrew Morton. He was stepping away from the Linux memory management subsystem, the part of the kernel responsible for deciding which programs get RAM, how much, and when to take it back. He had carried it since before it was even considered its own subsystem. Before Morton touched the Linux kernel, he was building kit computers in Sydney. He designed its best-known product, the Applix 1616, a Motorola 68000-based machine sold as a DIY kit through electronics magazines. It ran a custom multitasking operating system burned into ROM, a read-only memory chip that holds its data even when the power is off. He spent the next decade at Nortel Networks managing software development in Australia, then moved his family from Wollongong to Palo Alto in 2001.
